## 3. How we will work together

These are the **minimum** ways of working for the team and the client. They can be strengthened for a given project, but not relaxed.

### Iterations

We work in short, repeating cycles. Each cycle has three steps, in this order:

1. **Specification** — write or update the requirements, based on the agreed TOR.
2. **Research** — study options that can meet those requirements, and record the choice we make.
3. **Prototype** — design and build a prototype from that research, and evaluate it against the requirements.

How we use the time inside a week is up to the team. Progress is shown by a completed cycle and its evidence, not by hours reported.

### Weekly progress measure

We complete at least one full Specification → Research → Prototype cycle each week. If we do, that week is on plan. If we do not, that week is behind.

### End of each cycle

At the end of each cycle we answer two questions:

1. Does the prototype meet the current requirements?
2. Do those requirements still align with the agreed TOR?

If both answers are yes, that part of the work is done. If not, we improve one of two things:

- **Requirements gap** — the requirements are wrong, incomplete, unclear, or out of line with the TOR, or the prototype showed that. We revise the requirements. We do not change the agreed TOR; a change to the TOR means starting a new project.
- **Design gap** — the requirements are right, but the prototype does not yet meet them. We revise the design or the prototype.

Each cycle should shrink the gap between the current prototype and the result described in the TOR. We track remaining in-scope items; that list should get shorter, and it must never gain an out-of-scope item.

## 5. Economic evaluation

This section is used to decide whether a contract is financially worthwhile, and to set a price that is commercially reasonable, profitable for us, and justifiable to the customer. The price balances three factors: (a) our delivery cost, (b) our profitability, and (c) what the customer can afford. It is a planning evaluation, not an invoice.

The unit of effort is **person-days**. Final person-day estimates and economic assumptions must come from a named human. An agent may list the work required and may suggest task durations, clearly marked as suggestions, but must never produce the final figures itself; it must ask the human for them.
